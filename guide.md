# Cold outreach: what the evidence says

## Curiosity strategies for people who did not answer

These apply to every follow-up to someone who has not replied. The goal is to **give value or a concrete reason to answer**, never to demand an answer.

Rules:

- **One strategy per message**, different from those already used with that contact. Re-read what you already sent so you do not repeat text or data.
- **Write as a continuation of the conversation**, not as a reminder. "Just following up" is out: each touch brings something new. (Instantly 2026: a second touch written as a reply earned about 30% more than a formal reminder.)
- Everything verifiable and checked today. No invented cases, numbers or clients, no criticism of the contact, **no fear or threats**. Loss framing has a small, inconsistent effect in the research; a neutral "observation from today" is safer.
- Short and simple: 2 to 4 lines, everyday words, **one question only**, a question about interest (not about a meeting).
- After sending, log which strategy was used, so you can learn which ones earn replies (decide after 15 to 20 sends per strategy).

| Name | What it does | Principle | Example (swap in today's real data) |
|---|---|---|---|
| `new-observation` | A second stable fact the first message did not mention | Information gap (Loewenstein): curiosity is highest when the person already knows part of the picture and the missing part looks easy to close | "[Name], one more thing I noticed: your profile lists [category] as primary, and the top three results list [other category]." |
| `free-tip` | A small, useful fix, asking nothing in return | Reciprocity (Cialdini): a personal, unexpected gift weighs more than a generic one | "[Name], quick tip: your profile says hours are [x] and your site says [y]. Search shows the profile's." |
| `screenshot` | Attach the screenshot of the search (proof without making them open anything) | Reciprocity and concrete proof: deliver value before asking | "[Name], here is the screen for the search '[query]' in [city], taken today at [time]." |
| `local-number` | A market number with a source (search volume, seasonality) | A "why now" trigger | "[Name], '[query]' had about [n] searches a month in [city] (source: [tool]). [Month] is usually the peak." |
| `unnamed-competitor` | A gap: someone nearby shows up instead, without saying who | Information gap (the missing piece is small and they want to close it) | "[Name], a business [n] km from you ranks first when people search [service]. With far fewer reviews." |
| `provocative-question` | A question that lets them notice the problem themselves, selling nothing | "Poke the bear" (Josh Braun): one sharp question instead of an argument | "[Name], when someone searches [service] in [city], do you know where you show up today?" |
| `easy-question` | A yes/no question about their business | Low-effort ask (Instantly 2026: simple binary questions) | "[Name], are you still taking [service] clients this year? I saw a lot of searches in [city]." |
| `season` | The right moment, true for the niche | A "why now" trigger | "[Name], [seasonal event] starts in [month]. Showing up early puts you ahead." |
| `real-scarcity` | Only if true (for example, you take one client per niche and city) | Real scarcity (Cialdini), never invented | "[Name], I'm starting with one [niche] in [city] on [date]. If it makes sense for you, tell me by [day]." |
| `no-oriented-question` | A question built to get an easy "no" that reopens the conversation | No-oriented questions (Chris Voss, *Never Split the Difference*): saying "no" gives control and is easier to answer | "[Name], should I assume this is not a priority for you right now?" |
| `open-door` | A light close with no pressure (last touch) | Touch limit (Belkins: 4 or more emails triple spam complaints) | "[Name], last message from me. If you ever want to see what shows up for people searching [service], just say so." |

A reasonable order when you have no better reason: touch 1 `new-observation` or `free-tip`; touch 2 `open-door`. After sending a video or document: day +1 `screenshot`, day +4 the offer with `unnamed-competitor` or `local-number`, day +7 `no-oriented-question`, day +10 `open-door`. Someone who answered your last message leaves this list: answer them with the whole conversation as context.

## The evidence behind the copy

Almost all findings come from **US B2B email**, not from chat apps with small-business owners. Treat them as hypotheses with a basis, and measure them with your own template, hook and strategy columns.

| Finding | Source | How to apply |
|---|---|---|
| In a cold first message, asking for **interest** books more meetings than asking for a time; once the person is in the conversation, a **specific time** wins (37% vs 25% for the interest ask) | Gong Labs, 304,174 emails, meeting booked within 10 days | First message asks permission to send something ("Can I send it?"); after the yes, offer two specific times immediately |
| Curiosity is greatest when the person already has part of the information and the missing part seems small; with no clue there is no curiosity | Loewenstein (1994), information-gap theory | One concrete fact about them plus "there's one more thing I'd rather show you". Never a vague mystery alone |
| Short helps, but little: 20 to 39 words had the best rate (4.5%), and under 100 words averaged 3.9% vs 3.7% above. List quality and offer matter more. Lavender reports writing quality earning 58% to 79% more replies in some audiences (the "3rd to 5th grade reading level" claim appears only in third-party retellings and was not verified in the report) | Hunter (34 million emails, 2022 to 2024), Instantly 2026, Lavender (231,818 emails, Feb 2026) | 4 to 6 lines for the first message, 2 to 4 for follow-ups, one question |
| About 42% of replies come from follow-ups; each touch should add new value, 3 to 4 days apart | Instantly 2026 | A new observation first, a gentle close last; use the strategies above |
| Too many touches raise complaints (4 or more emails triple spam reports) | Belkins | Cap the number of follow-ups |
| A small, personal, unexpected gift increases reciprocation (candy study: +3.3% with one candy, +20% with a second one offered personally) | Cialdini on reciprocity (reported by NPR) | `free-tip`, `screenshot`, and any free deliverable |
| Giving people a chance to say "no" makes replying easier | Chris Voss, *Never Split the Difference* | "If now isn't the time, just say no" and `no-oriented-question` |
| Fear and loss framing have a small, inconsistent effect | Meta-analysis of gain vs loss framing (N = 50,780; mean effect close to zero) | Neutral observation of today, never a threat ("you're losing customers") |
| Brazilians use WhatsApp to talk to businesses (82% already have) and dislike automated replies (59%) | Opinion Box 2025 (1,126 users in Brazil), via articles citing the survey | Use the channel your audience uses; personalise every message, never robotic boilerplate |

## How much each source is worth

Credibility, then relevance for a short chat message to a small-business owner:

| Source | Credibility | Relevance | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gong Labs (CTA) | high: primary data, 304 thousand emails | medium | B2B software email; the pattern (interest first, time later) is general |
| Instantly 2026 | medium: the vendor's own data | medium | mass cold email; the direction holds (new value per touch, short messages) |
| Hunter and Lavender | medium: own data; Lavender read through third parties | medium | email; "short and simple" matters even more in chat |
| Belkins | medium: vendor study | medium | touch limits; in chat, nagging bothers people even more |
| Loewenstein | high: reference academic paper | high | how curiosity works in anyone, in any channel |
| Cialdini (reciprocity) | high: academic research, here via NPR | high | giving value before asking works in any channel |
| Chris Voss | medium: book and experience, no controlled study | medium | a conversation technique that fits short messages |
| Josh Braun | low to medium: practitioner course, no data | medium | a question technique; use it as a hypothesis |
| Gain/loss framing meta-analysis | high: academic | low to medium | about health messages; a caution, not a rule |
| Opinion Box 2025 | medium: serious survey, read through third parties | high for the channel | Brazilian users in Brazil; habits travel with people |

**The most relevant source is your own result.** No study was found on chat-app outreach to small-business owners in a diaspora market. So every send should record template, hook and strategy, and decisions should come from 15 to 20 sends per variation. When your numbers contradict a source above, your numbers win.
